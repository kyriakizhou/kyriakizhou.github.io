Optimal Subscription Messaging

send-time optimization / llm evaluation / xgboost / intern mentorship

Motivation

One of my team's responsibilities is sending customers every kind of subscription lifecycle email. One of the most important is the billing error email. It tells a customer that their auto-renew payment did not go through and that they are about to lose their subscription. Push notifications and in-app banners carry the same message.

Subscriptions bring in billions of dollars for Apple every year at a very high gross margin. That makes winning back customers who churn involuntarily critical: these customers did not choose to leave, a payment just failed. Even a 0.1% increase in win-back rate is worth millions of dollars.

A lot about this email can be improved. The first is when we send it. Today the messaging worker picks the day but not the time, and it reschedules itself one to three days out, so emails go out at an essentially random timestamp on a drifting day. The second is cadence: how many messages we send and on which days. The third is the email content itself. This project covers send time and cadence; content is a separate follow-up A/B test.

We tried three ways of choosing the send time, each more personalized than the last. Phase 1 tests simple rules in production, such as a fixed local hour. Phase 2 tests whether an LLM can read a subscription's history and predict whether and when the customer will come back. Phase 3 trains an XGBoost model on past outcomes and uses it to compare all 24 send hours for the same subscriber.

What we found: most of the gap between good and bad send hours comes from which customers happen to get messaged at those hours, not from the hour itself. In the raw data, recovery is 11.3 percentage points higher at 5 AM than at 9 PM, but older subscriptions both recover more often and tend to get their messages at different hours. Holding subscription age, message delay, and storefront constant shrinks the gap to 3.1 points. 8 PM to 10 PM is the worst window under every adjustment, and a fixed 5 AM send is associated with a 1.65-point lift that still needs a live test.

The LLM turned out to be a good classifier of which customers will come back, but its send-time guesses are barely better than random, and it costs too much to run on every subscription. The XGBoost model scores about 36,000 subscriptions per second on one machine, so it can score the full population and let us test a send-time policy offline before committing to a live experiment.

I proposed this project for the 2026 Commerce Subscriptions intern program and mentored the intern who carried it out. Who built what is under Resources.

Methods

Baseline

The messaging worker is a daily, account-level job in the subscription scheduler. It looks at an account's subscriptions, sends whatever messages are due, and schedules its next run one to three days out. Some of those steps are not whole days, so the send time shifts every cycle. And because one worker sends every message type, changing its schedule changes all of them.

We measured the baseline over the 30 days before the first test pod started. During that window, sends were spread almost evenly across the hours of the day. Recovery and win-back rates vary a lot by storefront, so any comparison between pods has to hold the storefront mix constant. Some recovered subscriptions also lapse again later, so recovery is not the same thing as long-term retention.

Phase 1: Human Heuristics

Phase 1 starts with the simplest change we could ship: send at a sensible local hour instead of a random one. Few customers fix a payment method at 3:00 AM, for example. Every Phase 1 treatment depends on knowing the customer's local time.

Timezone-Aware Send Times

Each subscription record has a user timezone field, but the field has no standard format. We parse it and store the target send time as an offset from local midnight. The snippet below shows the calculation for a 9:00 AM target; it is an illustration, not the production code.

ZoneId zone = ZoneId.of("America/Los_Angeles");
long offsetFromLocalMidnightMillis = 9 * 60 * 60 * 1000L;

LocalDate scheduledLocalDate = scheduledActionDate.toInstant()
        .atZone(zone)
        .toLocalDate();

ZonedDateTime target = scheduledLocalDate
        .atStartOfDay(zone)
        .plus(offsetFromLocalMidnightMillis, ChronoUnit.MILLIS);

Most records store a fixed GMT offset, which does not follow Daylight Saving Time, so some users end up an hour off for half the year. When the value is empty or cannot be parsed, we fall back to the storefront timezone.

Treatments

Each treatment changes only the time of day; the existing cadence still picks the day.

  • Fixed time. Each test pod sends at a single local hour, from 6:00 AM to 12:00 AM.
  • Purchase-time replication. The worker sends at the local time when the newest subscription on the account was first purchased.
  • Likely Time. The worker sends at the circular mean of the times when the user bought a subscription or turned off auto-renew.

Likely Time places each signal on a 24-hour circle and adds the points as vectors. The direction of the sum gives the mean time, and its length divided by the number of signals gives a cluster strength between 0 and 1. The worker only uses the mean when there are at least two signals and the strength is above 0.7 (a second pod uses 0.8). The circular math matters: a plain average of 11:50 PM and 12:10 AM comes out to noon.

Cadence Experiments

The cadence treatments change which days in the recovery window get a message. Each cadence tests its own hypothesis and runs on its own pod.

In-Pod Control

Pods drift on their own, so comparing one pod to another is not reliable. Starting in late July, each send-time treatment applies only to even account IDs, and the odd account IDs in the same pod serve as the control.

Phase 2: LLM

A fixed hour treats every customer the same, even though customers manage their subscriptions at very different times of day. So we tested whether a large LLM, given a system prompt and anonymized subscription data, could pick a better send time than a uniform rule. It first had to show it could identify which subscriptions would be won back, and then predict the local time of that win-back.

We built a Python harness that runs the same cohort through different prompts, models, and reasoning settings (steps in the figure below). Three design choices keep the test fair:

  • Outcome mask. The model sees each subscription as it looked right before its most recent win-back. For every other subscription, the harness strips the same fields, so the two classes look alike.
  • Enrichment. The harness converts timestamps into local ISO 8601 strings and adds app names next to app IDs. This makes the data easier to read without adding new information.
  • Structured output. The model answers through a tool call with four fields: a win-back boolean, a send time in HH:MM format, and a reason for each.

The grader checks the boolean with an exact match. For the send time, it measures circular distance to the nearest real win-back time, so 11:50 PM and 12:10 AM count as 20 minutes apart.

Phase 3: Machine Learning

In Phase 2, most of the LLM's reasoning read like a list of engineered features: number of earlier win-backs, tenure, cancellation type. Phase 3 also reframes the question. Instead of forecasting when a subscriber will come back, it asks how the probability of recovery changes if the worker sends the message at a different local hour.

Data and Label

Each row is one billing-error episode. The action is the local hour of the first recovery message after the failure, and the outcome is whether the subscription recovers within 30 days, through either a renewal or a win-back event. Counting both matters: the event type only reflects when the payment went through, so a win-back-only label would mark many of the fastest recoveries as failures.

The cohort covers about 5 million subscriptions, balanced across pods and across 60-day snapshots from June 2025 to June 2026. It ends two days before the first test pod started, so no episode saw the new logic. We split the data by account into 3,286,180 training rows, 365,179 validation rows, and 913,018 test rows.

We found and fixed two data problems that would have skewed the results:

  • Sample bias. An early per-pod trim kept the first rows by subscription ID, and subscription IDs grow with signup date. The kept subscriptions recovered at 2.4 times the rate of the dropped ones, so we replaced the trim with a hash-based sample.
  • Leakage. Some fields on the open purchase row are only written after a recovery. We now read those fields from the previous closed purchase instead.

Model and Features

We use gradient-boosted trees in XGBoost 2.1.4. Trees pick up interactions and thresholds without explicit terms, and they handle missing values and high-cardinality categories natively. We did not benchmark other model families, so this is a reasoned choice rather than a measured one. The model has 99 feature modules in nine groups (Table A2 in the appendix), and every feature outside the send-time group uses only data from before the failure.

The send hour is treated as one of 24 categories, so a single split can group 10 PM, 11 PM, midnight, and 1 AM together. Features that summarize many timestamps use the same circular math as Likely Time. The reference model allows up to 2,500 trees at depth 12, and early stopping ends training at around 2,460 trees.

Send-Hour Sweep

We score each held-out subscription 24 times, once per candidate local hour, changing only the send hour and its delay from the failure. If a candidate hour falls before the failure, the sweep moves the message to that hour on the next day. Scoring at the actual hour reproduces the original prediction for every row.

Because the production schedule did not assign hours at random, the sweep gives a conditional expectation, not a causal effect. As a control, we also sweep the local hour of the billing retry. No message is tied to that hour, so any variation there points to a time-of-day association the model failed to absorb.

Results

Human Heuristics

Operational fixes cut the scheduler lag from hours to about one minute.

A fixed local hour lands every account in a timezone on the same moment, and some test pods fell several hours behind. The timezone mix was not the cause: every badly lagging pod was already lagging before the experiments began. We moved those treatments to low-lag pods and added a 30-minute jitter. We also turned on buddy pods, which let disaster-recovery pods pick up work for busy primary pods.

The treatments are still live, with an in-pod control since late July, so their recovery results are not on this page yet.

LLM

We evaluated 13,997 subscriptions across two cohorts. The General cohort covers accounts with at least two subscriptions and at least one win-back event. The retry-heavy cohort also requires a billing-error event, which makes it closer to the recovery use case. We compared two system prompts with the same model and grader: a minimalist prompt that only states the task, and a handcrafted prompt that adds short examples of patterns we had verified at scale.

The handcrafted prompt is well calibrated and classifies best on the retry-heavy cohort.

The minimalist prompt predicts a win-back for about 44% of subscriptions in both cohorts, far above the true base rates. The handcrafted prompt stays close to the base rate and reaches 0.84 precision and 0.86 recall on the retry-heavy cohort. On the General cohort, the minimalist prompt does worse than always guessing no. When the two prompts disagree, the handcrafted one is right 88.6% of the time.

Send-time predictions barely beat a random guess.

A prediction counts as a hit if it lands within two hours of a real win-back time, so a random guess scores about 16.7%. The two prompts score 22.6% and 23.5%, but a fixed 5 PM rule scores 25.4%. Each prompt ends up applying one assumption to the whole population: the handcrafted prompt favors 9 AM to 1 PM, and the minimalist prompt favors 7 PM to 9 PM.

More reasoning does not help, and the cost is too high for production.

The large LLM's accuracy is flat from low to high reasoning (75.9% to 76.2%) and drops to 71.8% at the maximum setting. The smaller LLM drops from 69.4% to 65.8%. The full evaluation took 28,282 calls and cost about $658 at public list prices. Median latency is 5.6 seconds, but p99 latency is 53.5 seconds. The LLM is a good tool for testing hypotheses and generating training labels, but not for serving the full population.

Specific evidence makes a prediction trustworthy.

The model's stated reason is a good guide to how much to trust its prediction. Reasons that cite an open billing failure or a similar earlier recovery are right about 90% of the time, while reasons that cite only an earlier win-back are much weaker. Positive predictions backed by two different signals are also more precise than those backed by one.

  • Blind spot. Card-expiry cases make up about 62% of missed win-backs, because the model often treats an expired card as the end of the subscription.
  • Mirror errors. About 82% of false positives over-trust earlier win-backs, and about 82% of false negatives over-trust voluntary cancellations.
  • Long histories. On the longest histories, the false-positive rate climbs to about 50%.
  • Repeatability. Given identical input, the decision flips in about 5% of repeated calls.

Machine Learning

The model is well calibrated.

Table 1: Model quality on 913,018 held-out episodes; no test-set account appears in the training set. Lower test loss and higher ROC AUC are better, and bold marks the best value.

Across ten bins of predicted probability, the average gap between predicted and observed recovery is 0.22 percentage points. In other words, a predicted 0.7 really does mean recovery in about seven of ten cases.

Most of the raw send-hour spread comes from who gets the message.

In the raw data, first messages sent at 5 AM recover 11.3 points more often than those sent at 9 PM. But the baseline schedule did not choose hours, and the hours correlate with who the subscriber is. Messages at 4 AM go to subscriptions with more than twice the median age of those messaged at 9 PM, and older subscriptions recover more often. Across hours, median subscription age correlates with recovery at 0.93.

Two independent methods remove most of the spread. Re-weighting each hour to the same subscription mix shrinks it to 3.1 points. In the model sweep, the spread shrinks as the model learns more about the subscriber, from 9.4 points with two inputs to 2.7 points with 94 inputs.

Do not send at 9 PM.

The practical takeaway is which hours to avoid. Under every adjustment, the morning peak flattens into a broad daytime plateau, and 8 PM to 10 PM stays about 3 points below it. The model picks 5 AM most often, but the live sweep never tested that hour. If the worker sent every first message at 5 AM, the 94-input model predicts a 1.65-point lift.

Picking each subscriber's best hour gives 3.41 points, but taking the highest of 24 predictions is biased upward, so 1.65 points is the number to quote. It is an association, not a measured effect, and only a live test can confirm it.

The send hour is stronger than the control, but the control is weak.

In the 94-input model, the per-subscriber range is 6.5 points for the send hour versus 1.4 points for the retry hour. The control is weak evidence, though: the send-hour sweep also moves the delay, and the control sweep scores combinations that never appear in the training data.

If the hour matters, it matters for a small group.

For the median subscription, the best hour beats the worst by 6.0 points under the 94-input model, versus 11.8 points under the 14-input model. The same subscribers look less sensitive to timing once the model knows more about them. So if personalization is worth anything, the value sits in a minority with steep curves, and the model can produce a list of exactly those subscribers.

The model is cheap to run.

The model is a single 327 MB file. It scores about 36,000 subscriptions per second on one eight-thread machine, and all 92 training runs took 117 hours of local CPU time. It returns a calibrated number but no explanation, and for choosing a send hour, an explanation is not needed.

Limitations

  • Online results. The send-time treatments are still live, and the cadence treatments do not have an in-pod control.
  • Untested hours. The live sweep skipped 1 AM to 5 AM, yet the model prefers 5 AM. A live 5 AM test is the next step.
  • Causality. The send-hour sweep measures an association, not a causal effect.
  • Timezone accuracy. GMT offsets ignore DST; purchase records would give a more accurate timezone.
  • LLM grader. The send-time grader only accepts times close to a single observed event, which makes it strict.
  • Message content. This project only changes when a message is sent. A follow-up A/B test changes the wording of the billing-retry push.

Likely Time Calculation

strength = √( (Σ cos θi)² + (Σ sin θi)² ) / n
mean hour = (24 / 2π) · atan2( Σ sin θi, Σ cos θi ) mod 24

In these equations, θi = 2π × hour / 24 for each signal, and n is the number of signals. The worked example below uses three signals from a user in US Pacific time: 7:45 PM (19.75 hours), 8:05 PM (20.083 hours), and 8:20 PM (20.333 hours).

  1. Convert each time to a point on the unit circle. For 7:45 PM, the angle is 2π × 19.75 / 24 = 296.25°, x = cos 296.25° = 0.442, and y = sin 296.25° = −0.897. For 8:05 PM, the angle is 301.25°, x = 0.519, and y = −0.855. For 8:20 PM, the angle is 305.00°, x = 0.574, and y = −0.819.
  2. Add the vectors. The sum of the cosines is 0.442 + 0.519 + 0.574 = 1.535. The sum of the sines is −0.897 − 0.855 − 0.819 = −2.571.
  3. Calculate the cluster strength. The length is √(1.535² + 2.571²) = 2.994, and the strength is 2.994 / 3 = 0.998. That is above 0.7, so the algorithm continues.
  4. Calculate the circular mean. atan2(−2.571, 1.535) is −59.2°, which is 300.8° on the circle, or 20.056 hours. That is about 8:03 PM local time, which becomes the user's send time.
